Electrocardiographic evidence of left ventricular hypertrophy, identified by voltage criteria such as Sokolow-Lyon or Cornell and often accompanied by repolarisation "strain" changes, is strongly associated with long-standing hypertension and independently predicts cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. In the Framingham Heart Study, electrocardiographic left ventricular hypertrophy conferred a several-fold increase in coronary events and sudden death compared with a normal ECG.

Non-specific resting ST-segment or T-wave abnormalities are associated with higher rates of cardiovascular events and reduce the specificity of exercise ECG interpretation because baseline repolarisation changes can mimic or mask exercise-induced ischaemia. A normal resting ECG lowers, but does not exclude, the probability of coronary artery disease; many patients with obstructive disease have a normal ECG at rest.
