The classic Diamond and Forrester classification defines typical angina by three features: substernal chest discomfort, provoked by exertion or emotional stress, and relieved by rest or nitroglycerin. Atypical angina meets two of the three features, and non-anginal chest pain meets one or none. This classification underpins the chest pain categories used in the Cleveland Heart Disease data set.

The 2021 AHA/ACC chest pain guideline recommends describing chest pain as cardiac, possibly cardiac or non-cardiac and discourages the label "atypical" because presentations vary widely, especially in women, older adults and people with diabetes. Chest pain is one of the most common reasons for emergency department visits; most presentations are not acute coronary syndromes, but high-risk features such as pain at rest, haemodynamic instability or ischaemic ECG changes require immediate evaluation.

In the Cleveland Clinic cohort that trains this model, patients recorded as asymptomatic at the time of angiography had the highest prevalence of obstructive coronary disease, whereas typical angina was uncommon. This pattern reflects a population selected for coronary angiography, often with silent ischaemia demonstrated on prior testing, and should not be generalised to unselected patients presenting with chest pain.
