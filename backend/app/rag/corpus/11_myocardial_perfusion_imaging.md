Stress myocardial perfusion imaging, historically performed with thallium-201 and now mostly with technetium-99m agents, compares tracer uptake in the heart muscle at stress and at rest. A reversible defect, meaning reduced uptake during stress that normalises at rest, indicates inducible ischaemia from a flow-limiting coronary stenosis. A fixed defect present both at stress and at rest usually indicates infarct scar, although some fixed defects represent hibernating but viable myocardium.

The extent and severity of perfusion abnormalities stratify risk. A normal stress perfusion study predicts a low annual rate, below 1 percent, of cardiac death or myocardial infarction, whereas large or multiple reversible defects identify patients at substantially higher risk who are more likely to benefit from coronary angiography and revascularisation. In the Cleveland data set a reversible defect was the thallium finding most strongly associated with angiographic disease.
